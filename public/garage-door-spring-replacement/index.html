Torsion Springs vs. Extension Springs

Torsion Springs

Torsion springs mount horizontally above the door on a metal shaft and twist to store energy, releasing it evenly as the door opens. Most garage doors installed in the last 20+ years use torsion springs because they last longer, wind more predictably, and put less strain on the opener. Valiant's DuraMaster and Endurance Max torsion springs are powder-coated, high-cycle upgrades to this standard setup.

Extension Springs

Extension springs run along the horizontal tracks on either side of the door and stretch to store energy. They're common on older garage doors and some Wayne Dalton systems. Extension springs require a separate safety cable running through the coil — without it, a broken spring can become a projectile. Valiant checks for a working safety cable on every extension-spring job and can convert an unsafe setup to a torsion system.

Which One Do I Have?

If the spring sits directly above the door on a shaft, it's torsion. If it runs along the upper track and stretches when the door closes, it's extension. Valentino identifies the spring type, cycle rating, and any safety gaps during the initial visit, before quoting the repair.

Warning Signs You Need Spring Replacement

Loud Bang Or Pop

A sudden loud bang from the garage is almost always a torsion or extension spring snapping under tension. Stop using the opener immediately and call for service.

Door Feels Heavy Or Falls Fast

If the door feels noticeably heavier when lifted manually, or drops faster than normal when disconnected from the opener, the spring is losing tension and nearing failure.

Visible Gap In The Coil

A visible gap or separation in the torsion spring coil means it has already broken, even if the door still appears to move.

Opener Straining Or Grinding

An opener that strains, grinds, or reverses unexpectedly is often compensating for a weakened spring rather than an opener problem on its own.

Uneven Or Crooked Closing

A door that closes unevenly or looks crooked on one side can point to one spring failing while the other still holds tension — common on double-spring systems.

Frayed Cables Near The Spring

Cables that look frayed, kinked, or rusted near the spring assembly are a related failure point and should be inspected at the same time as the spring.
