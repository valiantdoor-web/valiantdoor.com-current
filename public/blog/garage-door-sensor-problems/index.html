Garage Door Sensor Problems

Safety sensors are simple: one eye sends a beam, the other receives it. Most sensor calls are solved by reading the little LEDs correctly instead of replacing parts.

Start By Reading The LEDs

Both sensors have a small indicator light. Those two lights are your diagnostic tool, and checking them takes ten seconds. Brands differ, so confirm against your opener's label, but the general pattern holds.

Sending eyeReceiving eyeRead it as
Steady onSteady onBeam is good, look elsewhere for the fault
Steady onOffBlocked, badly misaligned, or the receiver is dead
Steady onFlickeringMarginal alignment, dirty lens, or a loose wire
OffOffNo power to the pair: wiring or opener terminal

Clean The Lenses The Gentle Way

Dust films over these lenses quickly in a garage. Wipe each lens with a dry microfiber cloth or a clean cotton cloth. That is it.

Skip glass cleaner, solvents, and compressed air aimed at the housing. Liquids seep into the case and kill the electronics, and a hard blast of air can shift the alignment you were trying to preserve. Also brush away spider webs, which are a surprisingly common cause of a flickering receiver.

Realigning Without Guessing

Alignment means both eyes are at the same height and pointed straight at each other, four to six inches above the floor.

  1. Measure the height of each lens from the floor and match them.
  2. Loosen the bracket screw just enough to move the sensor by hand.
  3. Nudge it slowly while watching the receiver LED, and stop at the center of the range where it stays solid, not the edge.
  4. Snug the screw and check the LED again, since tightening often moves it.
  5. Wave a broom handle through the beam. The LED should drop out and recover cleanly.

If the light only holds when you press the sensor against the bracket, the bracket itself is bent or loose. Fix the bracket rather than fighting the aim.

Brackets, Mounts, And Bumps

Sensor brackets sit at bumper height in a busy garage. A cart, a mower, a bike pedal, or a car door knocks them out of aim constantly, and cheap stamped brackets stay bent once bumped.

Check that each bracket is fastened to the vertical track or the jamb, not dangling from a single loose screw. Rusted brackets in a damp garage lose their grip and let the sensor drift a little each week, which is why some doors get flaky before they fail outright.

When The Wiring Is The Real Problem

Two thin low-voltage wires run from each sensor up to the opener head. That wire gets stapled to framing, run past nails, chewed by rodents, and pinched by storage racks.

Look for staples driven through insulation, splices wrapped in tape, green corrosion at the terminal screws on the opener, and wires resting on a light fixture. If both LEDs are dark, wiring or the opener terminal is the likely fault, not the sensors.

Low voltage still deserves respect. Unplug the opener before touching the terminals, and if a wire run is damaged inside the ceiling, that is a job for a technician who can trace and replace it properly. See safety sensor service for what that involves.

Sunlight, Reflection, And Time Of Day

If the door refuses to close only during a specific hour, sunlight is the suspect. Low western sun in the late afternoon can saturate the receiving lens so it cannot pick out its partner's beam.

Which side is receiving matters. Moving the receiver to the shadier side of the opening, adding a short shade tube, or angling the bracket a few degrees away from the glare usually clears it. Bright white reflective flooring or a polished car parked just outside can also scatter light into the lens.

Test It Like A Technician

Once the lights look right, prove the system works instead of assuming it does.

  • Beam test: start the door down, then break the beam with a broom handle. The door should stop and reverse fully.
  • Contact test: lay a two-by-four flat on the floor under the door and close. The door should touch it and reverse.
  • Repeat test: run the door five times in a row. Intermittent faults hide behind a single successful cycle.

If either safety test fails, stop using the door for daily traffic until it is repaired. That is the whole point of the system.

When It Is Not The Sensors At All

Plenty of calls that start as sensor complaints end somewhere else. A door binding in a bent track reverses like an obstruction. A down travel limit set too deep makes the door bounce off the floor. A logic board fault can report a sensor error with perfectly aligned eyes.

If the LEDs are steady and the beam test passes but the door still refuses to close, move on to travel limits and opener behavior. Our will-not-close guide and LiftMaster troubleshooting pages pick up from there.

Sensor Questions

What do the sensor lights mean?

Broadly, a steady light on both units means the pair is talking. A dark or flickering light on the receiver means the beam is blocked, misaligned, or the wiring is bad. The sending unit's light usually stays on whenever it has power.

Can I just disconnect the sensors?

No. Photo eyes are a required safety feature on openers sold since 1993, and bypassing them removes the protection that stops a closing door on a child, a pet, or a bumper. If they will not work, fix them.

Do sensors go bad or is it always alignment?

Alignment, dirt, and wiring account for most cases. Sensors themselves do fail, usually after water intrusion, an impact, or a nearby power surge, and they are replaced as a matched pair.

Why do my sensors fail only in bright sun?

Direct low sunlight into the receiver lens can swamp the beam signal. A shield, a slight rotation of the bracket, or a sensor with better optics resolves it.
