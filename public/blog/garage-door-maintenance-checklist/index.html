Garage Door Maintenance Checklist

A garage door is the heaviest moving object in most homes, and it rewards a few minutes of attention on a schedule. Here is the checklist we give homeowners, split by how often each item is worth doing.

Monthly: Five Minutes, No Tools

  • Watch and listen to one full cycle. Stand inside and run the door up and down once. Note any new sound, hesitation, or shudder.
  • Check the door is level. Look at the gap along the top edge and both bottom corners with the door closed.
  • Test the photo eyes. Start the door down, then break the beam with a broom handle. It should reverse fully.
  • Test the contact reverse. Lay a two-by-four flat on the floor under the door and close it. The door should touch and reverse.
  • Clear the floor track line. Sweep grit, leaves, and gravel out of the channel where the door meets the concrete.
  • Check the bottom seal. Look for tears, hardening, or daylight along the closed edge.

If the reverse tests fail, that is not a wait-and-see item. Those systems are what keep a closing door from hurting someone.

Quarterly: Twenty Minutes, Light Tools

  • Lubricate correctly. Garage-door silicone or white lithium spray on hinge pins, roller bearings, and the torsion spring coils. Wipe the excess. Nothing on the track faces.
  • Snug visible hardware. Hinge bolts and roller bracket nuts, with the door closed and the opener unplugged. Firm, not forced. Skip the bottom corner brackets entirely, since they are under cable tension.
  • Inspect rollers. Spin each one you can reach. It should turn freely. Flat spots, wobble, and grinding all mean replacement.
  • Run the balance test. Disconnect the opener with the door closed, lift by hand to waist height, and let go. It should hold roughly in place.
  • Look over the cables. Flashlight only, hands off. Rust, broken strands, or slack means stop and call.
  • Test the opener battery backup. If your unit has one, unplug the opener and confirm it still cycles.

Annual: Owner Items Plus A Professional Visit

  • Wash the door face. Mild soap and water. It removes grit that dulls the finish and hides dents.
  • Check weatherstripping all around. Sides and top, not just the bottom.
  • Replace remote and keypad batteries on a set date so you are not doing it in the rain.
  • Reprogram or delete old remotes if you sold a car or changed tenants.
  • Book a professional tune-up. Balance measurement, spring inspection, cable and drum check, track alignment, roller replacement as needed, opener force and travel settings, and safety verification.

What we do on a maintenance visit is listed in full on that page, including which parts we recommend replacing on condition rather than on age.

The dividing line is simple: if a part stores energy or carries the door's weight, leave it alone. Everything else on the list is fair game for a careful homeowner.

Seasonal Notes For East Bay Garages

Winter rain is what hurts hardware here. Water running toward the door rusts bottom brackets and cable ends first. Before the rains, check that the driveway drains away from the door and that the bottom seal still makes contact.

Summer heat and afternoon glare bring the other pattern: sun-washed photo eyes on west-facing garages and expanded metal that binds slightly at the hottest hours. If a problem keeps a daily schedule, note the time when you call.

Keep A One-Page Door Log

Tape an index card inside the garage near the opener and write down four things: install year of the door, install year of the springs, date of the last professional service, and the opener model number.

Springs are rated in cycles, not years, so knowing when they went in tells you far more than guessing at the door's age. The opener model number saves a phone call when parts or remotes are needed.

Signs Maintenance Has Turned Into Repair

Stop the checklist and book service if you find any of these: a gap in a torsion spring, a frayed or slack cable, a roller outside its track, a door that will not hold position in the balance test, hardware you cannot keep tight, or a safety reverse that fails.

None of those are worse for waiting a day, but they all get worse with more cycles. Our noise guide is a good next read if a new sound is what put you on this page.

Why We Push Maintenance At All

Honest answer: it makes our emergency calls less dramatic. A door that gets looked at once a year tends to fail in a single part, on a weekday, with the car outside. A door that gets ignored for a decade tends to fail all at once, at night, with a bent panel involved.

Valiant is repair-first by design. Maintenance is the cheapest version of that philosophy, and it is the part you can do yourself between visits.

Maintenance Questions

How do I run a balance test?

With the door closed, pull the release cord to disconnect the opener, then lift the door by hand to about waist height and let go. A balanced door holds roughly in place. If it slides down or flies up, the springs need adjustment or replacement.

How often should a professional inspect the door?

Once a year for a typical family door. Twice a year if the door runs many cycles a day, serves a rental, or is a heavy wood or oversized custom door.

What should I never lubricate?

The track faces. Rollers are supposed to roll along clean, dry metal. Grease in the tracks collects grit and causes rollers to skid, which wears both parts faster.

Does maintenance actually extend spring life?

It helps. Springs fail on cycles, so nothing makes them permanent, but a door with free-rolling rollers and correct balance puts less strain on the whole system and avoids the secondary damage that turns one part into three.
